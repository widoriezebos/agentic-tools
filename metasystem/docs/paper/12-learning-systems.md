# 12. A System That Learns

**Recovering is not learning; a system has learned only when next time goes differently.**

In a continuation of the hypothetical session-expiry change, a later bounded release reaches a small share of live traffic on the weekend when clocks move forward. A few sessions expire at the wrong moment: one comparison uses local clock time while the rest use elapsed time. The releaser stops the expansion and restores the previous behavior. The danger passes, but the delivery system has not learned anything. It has only recovered.

Learning begins when experience changes what happens next time. A hurried ban on every use of local time would also stop legitimate work, such as showing an appointment in the user's time zone. A note in an incident report avoids that damage and is forgotten by the next builder. The useful lesson is a governed change to future behavior, supported by evidence and open to challenge.

## Incidents produce candidate lessons

The builder investigating the failure assembles the containment and observation records into an incident record that separates what was observed from what is suspected: the released version, the affected interval, the wrong behavior, the containment, the evidence preserved, the questions open. The separation matters because the first explanation after a failure is usually plausible and incomplete, and a wrong explanation turned into a rule is preserved with more force than a person's mistaken memory ever had. From the record, the builder proposes a candidate lesson: a security timeout must measure elapsed time, not local clock time, which jumps when clocks change. An independent examiner tries to reproduce the failure, looks for other causes and shows whether the lesson covers too much or too little.

A lesson can take two forms. Where a reliable check can tell the danger from legitimate work, the responsible authority may adopt it as an enforced rule. Where it concerns a value choice or a signal no check can recognize, it becomes a question or ruling for human judgment. The incident supplies evidence for either; it authorizes neither.

## Govern each enforced rule

Every incident tempts the team to leave a rule behind, and every enforced rule is power: it stops future work without asking anyone. Hundreds of unowned rules are the ceremonies of Chapter 4 come back as machinery, refusals that outlive their need with nobody left who can say why. So a rule is governed as the grant of power it is.

A rule is a handful of checked-in files: a check, which is code; any threshold it reads, which is data, and changing that value is an act of authority; the known-bad case the check must flag and the known-good cases it must pass; and a record. The check fires with power in one place, the acceptance boundary, against the exact candidate being submitted. The same check run by a builder while working is advice. The fixtures are Chapter 6's discriminating test pointed at the rule itself: not an argument, files that run. Application code is never the home of a rule; it is what the rules judge.

Engineering teams know this problem. Some of what they do is real governance (required owners, controlled changes, expiring exceptions), but the long tail of ordinary checks rests on team memory, and where nothing enforced reads a decision record, it can go stale without consequence. For a small, stable team on low-risk work that can be the cheapest adequate arrangement. It fails with turnover, leaving checks nobody understands, retained out of caution. It fails silently when the reason and the check drift apart. And it fails completely for a machine worker, which inherits no team memory and needs the record itself or a named route to ask.

So here the record is data, read by a rule about rules at the same acceptance boundary. No named owner, no known-bad fixture, no review date: no adoption. The owner is Chapter 5's legislator, whoever may maintain or withdraw the rule; the record also states what the rule may judge, the evidence a refusal must attach, the side-effect signals the owner watches and the appeal route, and the gate fills owner and appeal into every refusal. After adoption the gate keeps the rule honest: the known-bad fixture runs beside it, and when the fixture stops failing or the review date passes unrenewed, the gate withdraws the rule's refusal power, leaves it marking and tells the owner. Demotion is a term of adoption, not the rule changing itself. The pile of unowned refusals cannot form.

Every application-specific part of the metasystem is built the same way as the application itself: it enters as a feature on the backlog and is built, examined and accepted like any candidate. The difference comes after landing. The delivery records close: intent met, evidence bound, work done. The governance record stays open for as long as the rule holds power. A feature is finished when it lands; a rule holds power only while its record holds up.

Adoption is proportionate: a severe, well-reproduced recurrence may justify a firm rule; a weak association justifies observation only. The responsible authority decides what uncertainty is acceptable for the consequence at hand and records that reason. Like the ceremonies of Chapter 4, a rule stays only while the need it serves does; the review date and the appeal route are how it leaves.

## Test the enforced rule itself

The new check first runs marking only: it records what it would have stopped, and its sponsor reviews the marks. One mark is a false alarm, a calendar legitimately converting a timestamp for display. Another is a gap, a helper hiding the same unsafe comparison behind a different name. The gap shows what makes such a rule checkable at all: a check can only match what it can see. The durable repair is a narrower door: route all time reading through one module, and the rule becomes an import ban anyone can check. Adopting a rule often means shaping the application so the dangerous capability has one home.

The revised rule is then examined independently of its author, against changes that must be blocked and changes that must pass, and the whole rule set runs against the must-pass cases every rule keeps, because two sensible refusals can combine to block all valid work. Activation is gradual, recorded in the rule's record and advanced by its owner: warning, refusal in an isolated setting, refusal for a limited class, wider authority only while observations stay inside stated bounds. A passed trial is not permanent trust: the check may track a proxy that stops tracking the danger, a platform change may break its assumptions, builders may route around it. The skepticism applied to a software change applies to the mechanism judging changes.

## Floors, near-misses and human rulings

Months later a faster session implementation arrives, and one intentionally broken case shows it can revive an expired session. The existing check refuses it; nobody had to remember the original incident. The protection has become a tested floor a later change cannot cross unnoticed. A floor preserves a minimum, not a direction: shorter lifetimes are not automatically safer, more tests are not automatically better, and a floor can be repealed when the condition it protects no longer exists. The record makes that change reviewable instead of impossible.

In another region, observation shows sessions approaching the same faulty path, and release stops before anyone recorded loses access. The releaser records a near-miss and preserves the uncertainty (the exposure, the intervention, the missing evidence), claiming neither that nobody was harmed nor that harm occurred. Near-misses enter the same path as incidents, record to candidate lesson. If every alarming trace created a refusal, rules would grow faster than anyone could examine them; if only proven damage counted, the system would discard warnings it got free of harm.

Some lessons cannot become refusals at all. Support data shows more sign-ins, and accessibility representatives explain that repeated authentication burdens some users unequally. No check can decide how much burden a shorter session is worth; that is a value choice about the distribution of harm. Machinery gathers the rates, compares alternatives and shows where evidence is weak. The ruling belongs to the responsible authority, recorded with reasons, scope and a reconsideration date watched like a rule's review date, and open to challenge by the people it affects. Lessons too weak or rare for a reliable check stay in marking mode until repetition supplies the evidence; treating an unreliable test as settled would hide uncertainty, not reduce it.

## Repair, learn and revisit

Care comes first throughout. Containment does not wait for the lesson: the releaser restores the safe version while the responsible authority is told of possible harm, because delaying repair until every cause is known would make learning compete with care. Then the builder corrects the comparison, an independent examiner challenges the repair, and the custodian accepts the repaired candidate only with its own passing evidence. Production judges the results last: if the clock failure recurs, the rule's evidence no longer supports its authority; if legitimate work is blocked, its scope is wrong; if affected people overturn the reauthentication balance, the ruling changes. A system has not learned because it gathered prohibitions. It has learned when recorded experience changes future behavior in a way that can itself be revised.
